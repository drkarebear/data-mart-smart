From finding a number to using it well

From Data to a Conversation

You found something interesting. The next step is not to jump to a conclusion. It is to turn the result into a better institutional question.

Data are for inquiry, not a scorecard

A higher or lower number is not automatically evidence of a better or worse program, instructor, department, or college. Statewide data are strongest when they help identify patterns worth understanding. Professional judgment, local context, student experience, and other evidence may be needed to understand why a pattern exists.

A six-step inquiry process

  1. Describe what you see without explaining it. Start with the observation the data actually support.
  2. Check the source, measure, and comparison. Confirm the population, timeframe, program definition, and denominator.
  3. Ask what the data cannot tell you. A descriptive pattern rarely establishes cause by itself.
  4. Bring in practitioner and student context. Faculty, classified professionals, counselors, administrators, and students may know what the chart cannot show.
  5. Identify the next question. Decide what needs to be investigated rather than making one metric answer everything.
  6. Choose the right source for the next question. The next answer might come from DataVista, Data Mart, local IR, scheduling data, surveys, focus groups, student feedback, or several sources together.

A department-meeting discussion template

  • What do we notice?
  • What surprises us?
  • What can this measure tell us?
  • What can it not tell us?
  • What local context might matter?
  • Whose perspective is missing?
  • What do we want to investigate next?

When to bring in Institutional Research

Local IR or another campus expert becomes especially important when you need student-level longitudinal analysis, locally defined populations, confidential information, causal analysis, data not reported statewide, or a high-stakes institutional conclusion.
