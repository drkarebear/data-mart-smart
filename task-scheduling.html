Task guide: scheduling and enrollment management

Should We Add, Reduce, or Move Sections?

Use historical Data Mart patterns to frame a scheduling question, then make the actual schedule decision with current local enrollment and student-demand information.

In a Hurry?

  1. Decide whether you are studying students, enrollments, sections, or FTES.
  2. Compare the same term across multiple years when seasonality matters.
  3. Look at sections and enrollment activity together.
  4. Check modality, course, and program changes that could distort the trend.
  5. Before changing the schedule, verify with live local enrollment, fill, waitlist, and cancellation data.

Evidence to Pull From Data Mart

  • Course Details for course-specific section counts
  • Credit Courses/Sections for broader offerings and enrollments
  • Annual/Term Student Count for distinct student participation
  • FTES and Distance Education FTES where appropriate
  • multi-year term-to-term trends

Evidence to Add From Your College

  • live enrollment and seat availability
  • fill rates and waitlists
  • section cancellations and additions
  • time-of-day and modality demand
  • student education-plan/pathway needs
  • faculty, room, lab, and modality constraints

Questions the Data Should Help You Ask

  • Did student demand change, or did our number of offered sections change?
  • Which courses or times are filling differently?
  • Is the apparent trend caused by a modality or curriculum change?
  • Are students unable to get a required course?
  • Would moving a section solve the problem better than adding one?

Do Not Let One Number Make the Decision

Data Mart is historical MIS reporting, not a live registration dashboard. Do not add or cancel current sections solely because of a lagged Data Mart trend.

When Data Mart Is Not Enough

Bring in your institutional research office or another appropriate local expert when the question requires student-level longitudinal analysis, a locally defined cohort, confidential information, live operational data, causal analysis, or a high-stakes institutional conclusion.

Method Language You Can Adapt

I used consistent historical Data Mart measures to identify patterns in sections, enrollments, and instructional activity. I treated those patterns as planning context and verified current scheduling decisions against live local enrollment, waitlist, cancellation, and pathway information.
